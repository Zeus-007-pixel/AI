12.1Why vectorize?

So far each input held one number. Dymos needs your physics at many points in time at once: these are the nodes. Instead of running the component 60 times, you give every input and output a length of num_nodes, and NumPy computes all of them in one go (Day 6). Three changes turn a component into a vectorized one:

  1. an option num_nodes (often shortened to nn)
  2. arrays of length nn as starting values, such as val=np.ones(nn)
  3. derivatives declared as diagonal (12.2)

12.2A vectorized component with sparse partials

New file d12_vector.py:

{{code:d12_vector.py}} {{out:d12_vector.py}}
self.options.declare('num_nodes', types=int, default=1)
The number of points. types=int makes OpenMDAO reject anything that isn't a whole number.
self.add_input('rho', val=np.ones(nn), …)
Starting value is an array of nn ones, so this input holds nn numbers. (Ones rather than zeros avoid dividing by zero in other components.)
ar = np.arange(nn)
[0, 1, 2, 3, 4] for nn = 5.
self.declare_partials('q', 'rho', rows=ar, cols=ar)
Declares which derivative entries are not zero. See the picture below.
partials['q', 'v'] = inputs['rho'] * inputs['v']
An array of nn values: one derivative per point, filling the declared diagonal entries.

With 5 points, the derivatives of the 5 values of q with respect to the 5 values of v form a 5×5 table (a Jacobian). But q at point 3 only depends on v at point 3, so only the diagonal is non-zero:

v[0] v[1] v[2] v[3] v[4] q[0] [ ■ . . . . ] ■ = ρ·v at that point q[1] [ . ■ . . . ] . = always zero q[2] [ . . ■ . . ] q[3] [ . . . ■ . ] rows = [0,1,2,3,4] q[4] [ . . . . ■ ] cols = [0,1,2,3,4]

rows and cols list the positions of the non-zero entries: (0,0), (1,1), and so on. With 100 nodes that's 100 numbers instead of 10 000. This sparsity is what makes Dymos fast.

12.3The easy way: declare_coloring

Writing rows, cols and compute_partials for every pair is work. One line lets OpenMDAO discover the sparsity itself and compute only the non-zero derivatives with complex step:

self.declare_coloring(wrt='*', method='cs')

It replaces declare_partials('*', '*', method='cs'). It's accurate and much faster than plain cs when there are many nodes. This is what the glider ODE uses. When you later need more speed, switch the important components to analytic partials.

12.4A Dymos ODE, run on its own

This is the exact component Dymos will use on Day 15. Dymos isn't involved yet: we just give it 3 states by hand and look at the rates. New file d12_glider_ode.py:

Everything here you already know:

  • Inputs are the states (h, v, gamma), the control (alpha) and the parameters (m, S), each with nn values.
  • Outputs are the rates of change of the states (x_dot, …), plus q, which we want to constrain later.
  • compute holds the flat-Earth equations of motion: the same physics as your Day 7 ODE, but vectorized.

Read the numbers physically. At point 0 the glider is level (γ = 0), so h_dot = 0. At points 1 and 2 it descends at 5°, so h_dot is negative. gamma_dot is positive everywhere, because lift exceeds the weight component and the flight path is curving upward.

  3. Five points. Change nn to 5 in d12_glider_ode.py and supply 5 values for every input. What error do you get if one input still has only 3 values?

    Show answer

    OpenMDAO refuses with ValueError: Failed to set value of 'v': cannot reshape array of size 3 into shape (5,). The number of values must match the input's shape. That's a useful safety check.
